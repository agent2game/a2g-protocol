## AI has always been part of games

Every enemy that ever chased, hid or flanked a player ran on decisions a person designed. Each generation of game AI kept that person in charge and gave characters a better way to choose:

- **1980:** Pac-Man's four ghosts each follow their own targeting rule, so one chases and another cuts you off.
- **1998 to 2005:** squads that take cover and flank, behavior trees in Halo 2, goal planners in F.E.A.R.
- **Now:** a model weighs the whole situation and picks one of the moves designers built, inside the rules the game enforces.

A2G is that next step, made open and engine-neutral. It does not generate art, voices or dialogue, and it does not replace the people who make games. Designers still decide what a character can do, programmers still decide what happens, and the original AI is still there as the [native AI](/glossary/#native-ai) that takes over on any failure.

## How it works

<Architecture />

<Steps>

1. **Describe the game once.** A [manifest](/glossary/#manifest) lists the world, entity types, what each entity observes and which actions exist.
2. **Ask for decisions.** The game sends observations for a batch of entities and the actions legal right now. The service asks a model to pick one per entity.
3. **Validate and execute.** The game checks each choice and performs it through its own code, or runs its [native AI](/glossary/#native-ai) if there is no valid, fresh decision.
4. **Report back.** The game reports how each decision ended, which becomes the entity's [memory](/glossary/#memory) for its next decision.
5. **Learn and ship** *(proposed)*. Decisions from playtests train a small [local policy](/design/local-policy/). The shipped game runs it offline, with no service and no per-decision cost.

</Steps>
